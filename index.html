Map a Biome as a System

Objective. Collaborate with your peers to create a one-page system diagram that applies the Ecosystem Approach (Web of Life perspective) to a real-world biome, mapping its components and a key sustainability challenge.

Instructions

1.Select a Biome or Ecosystem

Choose one of the following:

  • Desert
  • Tropical Rainforest
  • Temperate Forest
  • Tundra
  • Freshwater
  • Marine
  • Boreal Forest
  • Grassland
  • Ice Ecosystems

2.Map the System & Analyze a Challenge

Collaborate with the students around you and create a visual or diagram that addresses:

Ecological Characteristics: Note the dominant vegetation, typical precipitation patterns, and how temperature/rainfall shape the biome.

Trophic Levels: Illustrate the food chain from producers to apex predators.

System Interactions: Use arrows to clearly link the Social, Ecological/Natural, and Technological components of the system.

Sustainability Lens: Identify one current challenge (e.g., deforestation, climate disruption) or opportunity (e.g., rewilding, community conservation) and propose potential solutions.

3.Research & AI Guidelines

Sources: You are encouraged to reference external materials (like the What Are Ecosystems video) or other credible sources.

Generative AI: You may use AI to search for information, compile facts, or brainstorm. However, you must properly credit which specific parts were AI-generated. Do not use AI to generate the entire visual or illustration.

The bottom line: you should be able to explain everything — the terms, the information, and the summary — outlined in this visual diagram.

Submission Requirements

While this is a collaborative effort, every student must submit their own copy on Canvas before the deadline. Your submission must include Metadata — the title of your work and your name; The Diagram — your mapped Ecosystem Approach and summary; and an Explanation of what the diagram shows.

How this works

This tool builds the one-page diagram the assignment asks for: choose a biome, describe its climate and vegetation, map the pieces of its system and how they act on one another, then save the result as a PDF.

The three arrows
+ amplifies The two move together: more of the first means more of the second, and less of the first means less of the second.
− dampens The two move in opposite directions: more of the first means less of the second — and less of the first means more of the second.
⚡ energy flows to The food chain — who eats whom. Run these from your producers up to your apex predators.

Switch to Connect and drag from one piece to another to draw one; clicking an arrow already on the canvas cycles it through the three. Remove deletes pieces and arrows alike.

Two things worth knowing

Rename anything. Double-click a piece to retitle it — a desert's Producers might read saguaro & creosote. Each palette group also ends in a blank Custom piece for whatever the palette lacks. Renaming changes the label only, so the checks go on working.

A food chain is not a feedback loop. Energy arrows are deliberately left out of the loop count: energy up a chain is a sequence, not a circuit. The loops the panel reports come from the + and − arrows, which is where a system's behaviour lives.

Finishing

The panel on the right names the feedback loops it finds and ticks off the assignment checklist as you work. When the checklist is full, write your sustainability lens and explanation under Write-up, then Save as PDF. Every student submits their own copy.

The worked examples are there to be taken apart: Coral reef & fishing town is a full food chain wired into a fishing town, with two loops pulling against each other.

Nothing is uploaded or stored — your work lives in this browser tab until you save the PDF.
